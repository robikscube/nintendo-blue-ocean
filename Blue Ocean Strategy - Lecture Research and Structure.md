# Blue Ocean Strategy — Lecture research brief and storyboard
Based on: W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne, “Blue Ocean Strategy,” Harvard Business Review, October 2004 (attached 23-page PDF).
Research checked: 21 October 2026. Prepared for a ~50–55-minute university class.
Status: RESEARCH + LECTURE STRUCTURE, NOT A FINISHED SLIDE DECK.

## Thesis / teaching purpose
**A firm does not always have to win a larger share of an existing market; it may be able to change the rules of value creation, attract people the market has not served, and redefine its competitors.** This is a strategic hypothesis and managerial framework, not a guarantee of monopoly or lasting success.

Learning outcomes:
- Contrast red-ocean competition with blue-ocean market creation.
- Explain value innovation: buyer value, producer costs, pricing, and whole-system alignment.
- Diagnose an industry through noncustomers, alternatives, and a qualitative strategy canvas.
- Apply Eliminate–Reduce–Raise–Create (ERRC) to construct and critique a new proposition.
- Evaluate execution, business viability, imitation, evidence, and external shocks.

## Arc: “Why enter a dying industry?” -> “Change the customer/problem” -> “Test the model” -> “Can it last?”
**Act I: The strategic problem (slides 1–5, 11 min)**
1. **The industry nobody wanted to enter** (2m): Two visuals, a traditional circus and a Cirque du Soleil performance. Ask: “In 1984, what would be a winning circus strategy?” Audience predicts cheaper tickets/bigger animals/better clowns, then reveal the industry was declining. Visual: split-screen with photo credits. Source: HBR PDF pp. 2–3; official Cirque production media.
2. **Cirque du Soleil: a counterintuitive success** (3m): Quebec street performers founded Cirque in 1984; target adults and corporate clients willing to pay theater-like prices. Introduce the strategic mystery before defining any jargon. Note original article’s 22x revenue-growth figure describes its historical study window, NOT its current growth. Source: HBR PDF pp. 1–3.
3. **Red oceans vs. blue oceans** (2m): Red = compete for existing demand within given industry boundaries; blue = create new demand by reconstructing boundaries. Not “competition never comes back.” Visual: compact side-by-side comparison (derive from HBR figure on PDF p.14). Source: HBR PDF pp. 3–4, 14.
4. **Change the unit of strategy: company -> strategic move** (2m): The same company can make good and bad strategic moves; tech alone is not the definition of value innovation. Show Ford, Dell, Cirque as moves rather than “magic companies.” Source: HBR PDF pp. 11–13.
5. **The authors’ provocative data, with a warning label** (2m): In the authors’ study of launches in 108 firms, they classify 14% as market-creating and associate those launches with 61% of profits; 86% incremental launches with 39%. Show two 100% bars. Make explicit: descriptive results from their sample, not a forecast that any new blue-ocean attempt has 61% chance of profit. Source: HBR PDF p. 6.

**Act II: The mechanism (slides 6–11, 19m)**
6. **The central engine: value innovation** (4m): Simultaneously improve buyer value and reduce avoidable *producer* costs through activity redesign. Make clear Cirque sold tickets at higher prices, so “low cost” does not mean “lowest selling price.” Sketch: price -> buyer value and producer margin; costs saved on industry-orthodox factors, value invested in new factors. Source: HBR PDF pp.14–18; https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/tools/value-innovation/
7. **Dissect the Cirque move using ERRC** (4m): Display 2x2 grid:
   - Eliminate: animal acts, big-name performers, multiple rings, aisle concessions.
   - Reduce: emphasis on classic danger and slapstick acts (not abolish acrobatics).
   - Raise: artistic quality, audience comfort, coherence of performance.
   - Create: theatrical story/themes, original music, choreography, distinct productions, adult-oriented experience.
   Ask students which choices reduce cost and which create new willingness to pay. Note illustrative educational interpretation, not audited cost allocations. Source: HBR PDF pp.15–17; https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/tools/four-actions-framework/
8. **Strategy canvas: make the new value curve visible** (3m): X-axis: factors (animal shows, star performers, multiple rings, comfort, story, music, sophistication). Y-axis: relative offering level, NOT numeric sales or profitability. Contrast traditional circus and Cirque qualitative curves. Label any newly drawn curve as “illustrative”. Source: https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/tools/-Strategy-canvas/
9. **Look at noncustomers, not just customers** (3m): Three tiers: about-to-leave, consciously refuse, never-considered. Cirque drew theater/opera/ballet-goers who were not seeking a traditional circus. Ask what makes a noncustomer reject the category. Visual: three concentric circles. Source: https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/tools/three-tiers-of-noncustomers/ ; HBR PDF p.2.
10. **Market boundaries are choices: the Six Paths** (3m): Alternatives, strategic groups, buyer groups, complements, functional vs emotional orientation, trends over time. Focus on *two* paths in depth: circus vs theatrical entertainment and parents/buyers vs children/end-users. Source: https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/tools/six-paths-framework/ ; https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/teaching-materials/cirque-du-soleil/
11. **Ford Model T: the competitor was the horse** (2m): Cars were status objects; Ford redefined the target as everyday mobility and benchmarked against horse-drawn carriage affordability, reliability, repairability. Black standardized Model T/assembly-line changed economics. Caution: historical figures and comparison belong to the 2004 article. Source: HBR PDF pp.20–22.

**Act III: Does it generalize, and does it last? (slides 12–16, 16m)**
12. **Nintendo Wii: compete for the living room, not graphics benchmarks** (3m): Against a console industry's technical spec contest, Nintendo emphasized intuitive motion play and family/social participation, opening the audience to many nontraditional gamers. Source: https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/teaching-materials/nintendo-wii/ ; Nintendo historical marketing (verify rights).
13. **Test of durability: Wii -> Wii U** (3m): Official Nintendo lifetime worldwide hardware sales: Wii 101.63m versus Wii U 13.56m, reported as of 30 June 2026. This contrast is a provocation, not causal proof: sequel positioning, execution, and context differed. Blue oceans need renewal. Source: https://www.nintendo.co.jp/ir/en/finance/hard_soft/index.html
14. **2026 case prompt: is Liquid Death really a blue ocean?** (3m): Tall aluminum cans, dark humor, unconventional branding and positioning in bottled water. Ask: category creation or just excellent brand differentiation? Beware: startup valuation and social buzz do NOT demonstrate enduring profitability. The authors offer a new Liquid Death teaching case. Source: https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/teaching-materials/liquid-death/ . Optional alternate for AI-heavy classes: NVIDIA's 1999/2006/2016 strategic moves from the authors’ new case at https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/teaching-materials/nvidias-market-creating-blue-ocean-moves/
15. **Idea quality ≠ business viability** (3m): Test utility, strategic price, target cost, adoption barriers (people and channels); check whether gains accrue to buyers AND the firm. Visual: four gates; if any fails, revisit idea. Source: https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/tools/sequence-of-bos/ . Explicitly mark this as supplementary toolkit beyond the 2004 assigned essay.
16. **Blue oceans turn red, and firms remain vulnerable** (4m): Cirque emerged from Canadian CCAA / U.S. Chapter 15 creditor protection on 24 Nov 2020. That shock does not invalidate its 1980s market-making move, but it rebuts a simplistic “create a blue ocean, then relax” story. Contrast Kim/Mauborgne with Porter: competitive positioning and activity fit still matter; market-creating and competitive strategy can be complementary. Question survivorship bias in retrospective success cases. Sources: https://www.newswire.ca/news-releases/cirque-du-soleil-entertainment-group-confirms-closing-of-sale-transaction-and-emergence-from-creditor-protection-892218704.html ; https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=10698 ; https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2024822

**Act IV: Apply and synthesize (slides 17–18, 10m)**
17. **Class mini-lab: reinvent a campus career fair** (8m): Problem: students who avoid standard career fairs are your noncustomers. Groups of 3–4: (1) identify why they don't participate; (2) create ERRC grid with one item in each quadrant; (3) name ONE new group to attract; (4) predict one cost-saving mechanism; (5) write a concise value proposition. Two groups share; class votes if it is truly market-making or simple improvement. Visual: fill-in 2x2 ERRC template + 60-second timer.
18. **The synthesis: don't beat rivals at their game; ask which game matters** (2m): Three tests: Who are the noncustomers? Which competition factors are unnecessary? Is buyer value, cost, price, and adoption aligned? End on: “What will make your proposed blue ocean red?”

TOTAL: 56 minutes at the times above. For a 45-minute class cut slide 5, reduce slide 10 to 1m, slide 14 to a one-minute visual poll, slide 17 to 5m, and slide 16 to 3m.

## Discussion questions
1. Cirque sells premium tickets. In what sense is this a low-cost strategy?
2. Is an entirely new technology necessary for a blue ocean?
3. Is Liquid Death a fundamentally new market or an excellent repositioning in a red ocean?
4. Why was Nintendo unable to reproduce the Wii's success automatically with Wii U?
5. If a strategy creates new demand, how long until competitors imitate it?
6. Are “uncontested market space” and “competition irrelevant” literally sustainable, or rhetorical descriptions of a temporary opening?
