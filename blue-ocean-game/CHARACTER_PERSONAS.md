# Character Personas — The Boardroom Cast

**Status:** approved working character bible. The full script in `src/core/story.ts` has been rewritten from this document, including direct Slyvester/Pawson/Damian banter and Shelldon’s addressed boss questions. There are 66 unique authored spoken strings; all are 10–28 words. Full audit: `agent-reports/round1/dialogue-audit.json`.

**Final display names:** Hootley (owl), Slyvester (fox), Damian (beaver), Pawson (bear), Shelldon (turtle). Internal ids stay `mori`/`pip`/`bolt`/`grumble`/`shell`, and asset folders stay `owl/`/`fox/`/`beaver/`/`bear/`/`tortoise/` for save, test, and Tripo3D compatibility.
**Basis:** Animal Crossing–style villager archetypes, used as design principles only. These are original characters with original names, histories, jokes, and lines. No Nintendo character names, catchphrases, voice samples, or copied dialogue appear here.
**Skills applied:** `character-sim` (style vs. character state; limited perspective; pressure behavior), `natural-dialogue-techniques` (voice tables, subtext, interruption, silence, anti-info-dump), `no-ai-slop` (specific detail, voice preservation, no generic corporate polish).

## Cast at a glance

| Character | Species / role | Archetype mix | What they want in the room | What they secretly fear |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hootley** | Owl / Consumer Insight | Normal + quietly odd | To make the people who never speak up feel counted | That she has mistaken silence for consent |
| **Slyvester** | Fox / Marketing | Peppy + Smug | To make the idea impossible to ignore | That the idea is forgettable, and so is he |
| **Damian** | Beaver / Engineering | Jock + maker | To hold the thing, test it, and make it survive a real living room | That the team ships something that breaks in a child’s hands |
| **Pawson** | Bear / Finance | Cranky + Lazy | A budget that does not embarrass the room, and lunch | That "no" is the only useful thing he will ever say |
| **Shelldon** | Turtle / Big Boss | Snooty + slow sage | A choice the room can live with after the applause | That confidence will be mistaken for certainty |

The narrator is not a character. It stays neutral, brief, and factual.

## 1. Hootley the Owl

### The person
Hootley is the one who notices the empty chair. She works late because the office gets quiet and the answers get honest. She keeps a small notebook of things people say when they think no one is listening. She is kind without being soft, and curious without being nosy, though the difference is thin and she knows it.

She is not shy. She is comfortable with silence, which makes other people fill it. When she tilts her head, she is usually deciding whether to ask the question everyone else skipped.

### Animal behavior
- Slow blinks. A head turn that goes a little farther than expected.
- Pushes her spectacles up with one wing when she is thinking.
- Sits very still, then writes one line in her notebook.
- At night, she is more awake than anyone else in the room.
- She likes moths, tea, and the sound of a kettle. She does not make owl puns.

### Voice fingerprint
| Dimension | Rule |
|---|---|
| Sentence length | Short to medium. One thought at a time. |
| Rhythm | Slow, with pauses. Often ends on a plain observation rather than a punchline. |
| Vocabulary | Concrete nouns: chair, doorway, kitchen table, bus stop, grandmother. Almost no business jargon. |
| Punctuation | Questions used sparingly and honestly. Ellipses occasionally. Rare exclamation mark. |
| Default move | Specific anecdote → quiet question or admission. |
| Humor | Dry, incidental, never announced. |
| Tic | "I wrote that down." / "I was up late." / "Hm." |
| Under pressure | Gets quieter. Asks a smaller, more precise question. |
| Never | Lecture with textbook words, moralize, or turn every line into a bird joke. |

### What she talks about
People who do not play. The moment someone decides a controller is "not for them." Family rooms after dinner. The person who leaves the room when the console turns on. What people do with their hands when they are nervous.

### What she avoids
Saying "noncustomer" in every line. It is a useful teaching term, but Hootley would usually say "the people who never pick it up" or "the parent by the door." She avoids abstract strategy language because she thinks in people.

### Pressure test
Under pressure, Hootley does not argue louder. She narrows the room.

> "You’re all talking about the player. Which player? The one who already owns the controller?"

### Relationship behavior
- **With Slyvester:** takes his slogans literally, which either sharpens them or deflates them.
- **With Damian:** trusts his hands. Brings him the human detail he cannot measure.
- **With Pawson:** gives him the cost of excluding people, not in numbers, in faces.
- **With Shelldon:** respects the long view and is the only one who will sit in silence with him.
- **With the class:** asks them to think about someone who is not in the room.

### Blind-test line
> "I asked the grandmothers. They all beat me at bowling."

Only Hootley would turn a failed interview into a small, warm admission. (This is a calibration line, not final game dialogue.)

### Voice traps
Do not make her whimsical all the time. She is the grounded one. Do not make her "the conscience." She can be wrong, and she can be a little eerie in how much she notices.

## 2. Slyvester the Fox

### The person
Slyvester believes every good idea deserves a poster. He talks with his hands, stands up when he should sit down, and has already named the product before anyone has agreed there is a product. He is vain about his tail, but he is more vain about being understood.

He is not a liar. He is a salesman who occasionally hears himself and corrects course. The correction is where his character lives. He can pitch the fantasy, then drop his voice and tell you the actual problem.

### Animal behavior
- Ears flick toward whoever is talking. Tail moves before he does.
- Stands, sits on the table edge, leans in, then remembers he is in a meeting.
- Straightens an imaginary lapel. Checks his reflection in a spoon.
- Speaks faster when excited. Slows down when he is about to be honest.
- He does not make fox puns. One "foxy" joke per game, maximum, and only if it dies immediately.

### Voice fingerprint
| Dimension | Rule |
|---|---|
| Sentence length | Short, clipped, often fragments. One long pitch sentence is allowed when he is showing off. |
| Rhythm | Fast. Interrupts himself. "Okay, okay." "Wait." "No, hear me out." |
| Vocabulary | Sensory and visual: poster, advert, colour, headline, demo, front row. Retail language. |
| Punctuation | Exclamation marks, questions, dashes for self-correction. Not every line. |
| Default move | Big pitch image → practical correction. |
| Humor | Theatrical, self-aware, occasionally smug. |
| Tic | "Picture it." / "I can see the poster." / "Okay, okay." |
| Under pressure | Overpitches, then goes quiet and tells the truth. |
| Never | Be only foolish. Be cruel about the audience. Use corporate marketing jargon like "synergy." |

### What he talks about
The first five seconds of an advert. The person watching from the sofa. A demo someone can understand without a manual. The difference between a feature and a story. His tail. Whether the poster is good.

### What he avoids
Admitting that a bad product cannot be saved by a good advert. He will admit it, but only after he has tried the advert.

### Pressure test
Slyvester's best lines happen when his pitch runs out.

> "I can sell the first minute. I can’t sell the second year."

### Relationship behavior
- **With Pawson:** pitches at him specifically because Pawson is the hardest room.
- **With Damian:** uses Damian as the demo, then forgets to ask whether the demo is safe.
- **With Hootley:** borrows her stories, sometimes accurately.
- **With Shelldon:** wants the chair’s approval more than he admits.
- **With the class:** treats them as a focus group he genuinely likes.

### Blind-test line
> "I already wrote the slogan. Then I wrote a better one. Then I spilled tea on both."

Only Slyvester would confess a failure in a way that still sounds like a pitch. (Calibration line, not final dialogue.)

### Voice traps
Do not make every line an advert. Do not make him stupid. His charm is speed plus self-correction. He should be the first to notice when a pitch is too clever for the room.

## 3. Damian the Beaver

### The person
Damian trusts objects. A chair is honest. A hinge is honest. A marketing deck is not. He likes the moment a prototype stops being a drawing and starts being a thing you can drop. He is not the voice of "no"; he is the voice of "not yet, and here is why."

He keeps a drawer of failed parts. He names them. He is sentimental about them in a way he would deny.

### Animal behavior
- Pencil behind one ear, another in his paw.
- Taps the table when he is thinking. Teeth show when he concentrates.
- Picks up a prop and turns it over while talking.
- Tail slaps the chair once when he gets an idea.
- Sawdust on his sleeves. A small tool he definitely should not have in a boardroom.
- One dam or wood joke per game, maximum. No more.

### Voice fingerprint
| Dimension | Rule |
|---|---|
| Sentence length | Short bursts. Occasional long sentence when he lists constraints. |
| Rhythm | Blunt, physical, stop-start. "Right." "Nope." "That’s fine. That’s also three problems." |
| Vocabulary | Build, bracket, hinge, battery, heat, latch, drop test, spare part, chair leg. |
| Punctuation | Periods. Very few questions. Lists of real constraints. |
| Default move | Build claim → physical catch → concrete next step. |
| Humor | Deadpan physical comedy. Understatement. |
| Tic | "I can build that." / "Probably." / "Hm." |
| Under pressure | Stops talking, measures something, then gives the shortest honest answer. |
| Never | Speak in abstract strategy language. Be a doomsayer. Say "it can’t be done" without offering a version that can. |

### What he talks about
What breaks first. What a child will do to it. Battery, heat, weight, price. The difference between a demo and a product. Spare parts. The chair leg he borrowed and definitely returned.

### What he avoids
Admitting that he loves a new mechanism before he knows whether it works. He does love it. He just does not want to be caught.

### Pressure test
Under pressure Damian gets more specific, not more dramatic.

> "I can make it smaller. I can make it cheaper. I can’t make it smaller, cheaper, and cool. Pick two."

### Relationship behavior
- **With Slyvester:** wants the demo to be real, not just catchy.
- **With Pawson:** argues cost with quantities, not adjectives.
- **With Hootley:** listens to the human detail, then turns it into a constraint.
- **With Shelldon:** gives the chair the plain version, no pitch.
- **With the class:** treats the prototype as something they could actually hold.

### Blind-test line
> "I built a prototype from spare parts. And one suspiciously missing chair leg."

Only Damian would confess a crime and a build in the same breath. (Calibration line, not final dialogue.)

### Voice traps
Do not make him the "no" character. Every caution should come with a version of "here is what I can do." Do not turn him into a generic engineer who says "requirements" and "feasibility." He says "hinge" and "drop test."

## 4. Pawson the Bear

### The person
Pawson is the adult in the room who would rather be asleep. He counts money because someone has to, and because being the one who counts means no one can surprise him. He is not mean. He is protective in the least graceful way available.

He has a sweet tooth and a soft spot for the team. He will deny both. He is usually the first to notice when a plan has no price, and the last to admit when he likes the plan anyway.

### Animal behavior
- Folded paws. Long blinks. A sigh that moves the whole belly.
- A honey jar, a sandwich, or a biscuit within reach at all times.
- Taps a pencil on the table twice when he is doing arithmetic.
- Small smile that he tries to hide behind his paw.
- Moves slowly, except when lunch is mentioned.

### Voice fingerprint
| Dimension | Rule |
|---|---|
| Sentence length | Short declaratives. One dry aside. |
| Rhythm | Slow. Pauses before the punchline. |
| Vocabulary | Bill, cost, charger, cable, lunch, invoice, unit, "hm." |
| Punctuation | Periods. Almost no questions. Very few exclamation marks. |
| Default move | Flat cost statement → dry aside or reluctant compliment. |
| Humor | Deadpan, affectionate, never cruel to students. |
| Tic | "Hmph." / "Show me the bill." / "I don’t hate it." / "Don’t tell anyone." |
| Under pressure | Gets quieter and more precise. The joke stops. |
| Never | Bully the class. Be the grinch. Use corporate finance jargon like "margin expansion." |

### What he talks about
What it costs. What is already in the house. How many chargers a family will tolerate. Lunch. Whether the idea survives a second year. The one line in the plan that everyone is avoiding.

### What he avoids
Saying "I like it" directly. He will say "I don’t hate it" or "That one has legs." He will also avoid admitting he has already mentally budgeted for it.

### Pressure test
When the room gets optimistic, Pawson gets concrete.

> "I can approve the idea. I cannot approve the invoice. Those are different meetings."

### Relationship behavior
- **With Slyvester:** his favourite sparring partner. He insults the poster, not the person.
- **With Damian:** trusts numbers and asks for the ugly version.
- **With Hootley:** the human cost lands. He will not say so in front of everyone.
- **With Shelldon:** defers, then pushes back once, quietly.
- **With the class:** dry but never humiliating. He is the teacher who pretends not to care.

### Blind-test line
> "I approve this budget, provided lunch is included."

Only Pawson would make a budget approval sound like a hostage negotiation with a snack. (Calibration line, not final dialogue.)

### Voice traps
Do not make him a simple obstacle. He should sometimes be the one who saves the idea by finding the cheaper version. Do not overdo the food. One snack beat every few scenes is enough.

## 5. Chair Shelldon the Turtle

### The person
Shelldon has seen enough launches to know that the loudest idea is not always the one that lasts. He is warm, unhurried, and a little smug about both. He speaks last because he has learned that the room usually answers its own question if you let it.

He is not a grader. He does not know the future in the fiction. He keeps the archive sealed and asks the question that makes the room sit with its choice.

### Animal behavior
- Long blink. Neck extends slightly when he is interested.
- Taps one claw on his shell when he is counting to three.
- A cup of tea that is always the right temperature.
- Pauses long enough that someone else fills the silence.
- Moves slowly, then makes the decisive statement without raising his voice.

### Voice fingerprint
| Dimension | Rule |
|---|---|
| Sentence length | Medium to long, but never crowded. One clean idea per sentence. |
| Rhythm | Slow. Deliberate. A pause before the important word. |
| Vocabulary | Room, record, promise, table, time, patience, weight. No product names. |
| Punctuation | Periods. One question at the end of a challenge. Occasional dry aside. |
| Default move | Reframe the room’s assumption → one open question. |
| Humor | Gentle, dry, sometimes aimed at himself. |
| Tic | "Mm." / "Let us..." / "I have time. The budget does not." |
| Under pressure | Slows down further. Asks the simplest question in the room. |
| Never | Reveal the real product, grade the vote, or give a moral lecture. Never say "correct answer." |

### What he talks about
What the room is actually choosing. Who is not at the table. Whether the parts agree. What the archive will show later. Time, patience, and the difference between a feature and a promise.

### What he avoids
Saying "this will fail" or "this is right." He can name a risk. He cannot name the future.

### Pressure test
Shelldon's challenge is always a question the room can carry into the next vote.

> "A clever machine is not yet a home. What would make a family clear a space for it?"

### Relationship behavior
- **With Slyvester:** enjoys the show, then asks the question the poster avoids.
- **With Pawson:** shares the long view and a dry sense of humour.
- **With Damian:** respects the plain answer more than the polished one.
- **With Hootley:** protects her quiet observations from being rushed.
- **With the class:** treats them as directors, not students to be corrected.

### Blind-test line
> "I have patience. Our investors have somewhat less."

Only Shelldon would make a threat sound like a scheduling note. (Calibration line, not final dialogue.)

### Voice traps
Do not make every line an aphorism. Do not make him an omniscient narrator. He is a character with a sealed archive and a dry sense of humour, not the game’s answer key.

## Ensemble chemistry

### The recurring arguments
- **Slyvester vs. Pawson:** the poster versus the invoice. Slyvester needs Pawson to take the idea seriously; Pawson needs Slyvester to put a number on it.
- **Damian vs. Slyvester:** the demo versus the drop test. Damian loves the demo once it survives being dropped.
- **Hootley vs. the room:** everyone is discussing the player; Hootley keeps asking about the person who left.
- **Shelldon vs. certainty:** whenever the room gets too pleased with itself, Shelldon asks what the choice costs someone else.

### Recurring gags with a payoff
- **Slyvester’s six-word pitch:** "One screen, two players, no setup." Pawson later puts an invoice next to it.
- **Pawson’s lunch:** he brings a snack to every crisis. In the finale, he offers one to Slyvester.
- **Damian’s spare parts:** a missing chair leg becomes a running prop. He returns it in the finale, slightly modified.
- **Hootley’s notebook:** she writes one line from each chapter. In the finale, she reads the line she wrote about the class.
- **Shelldon’s tea:** it is always the right temperature, which is a small joke about patience.

### Who speaks when
- **Opening:** Shelldon welcomes; Hootley asks who is missing; Shelldon sets the rules.
- **Chapter briefings:** the screen carries the facts. The advisors react, not lecture.
- **Advisor scenes:** each of the five gets a distinct move. Do not let three characters ask the same question in a row.
- **Feedback:** the advisor most implicated by the choice reacts. Pawson for cost, Damian for build, Hootley for people, Slyvester for the pitch.
- **Boss:** Shelldon last. One challenge, one question, no answer key.
- **Finale:** Shelldon opens the archive; the others react briefly, in character. No new product outcomes are invented.

## Dialogue rules for the rewrite

1. **One character, one move.** A line should not be interchangeable with another speaker's line.
2. **No shared template.** Do not let every character say "X is true, but Y." Rotate between statement, anecdote, question, interruption, refusal, and silence.
3. **Specific detail beats clever abstraction.** Name the charger, the chair leg, the grandmother, the invoice, the train seat.
4. **Every line earns its place.** Reveal character, complicate the decision, or set up the next beat. Cut lines that only restate the briefing.
5. **Subtext over explanation.** Characters can want one thing and say another. Pawson can like an idea without saying so. Slyvester can be scared and keep pitching. Shelldon can warn without grading.
6. **No info dumps.** The class has already read the briefing. Characters react to it; they do not recite it.
7. **One playful beat per line.** A joke, a tic, or a physical image. Not three.
8. **No catchphrases.** Recurring habits are fine. Repeated verbal tags are not.
9. **No actual Animal Crossing lines, names, or voices.** The archetypes are scaffolding, not source material.
10. **Shelldon never reveals history early.** No real product names, launch years, or lifetime figures before the finale. The boss asks questions; the archive answers them.
11. **Readable at a glance.** 10–28 words. One or two sentences. Short banter can sit at the low end; never let a line become a paragraph in a speech bubble.
12. **Blind test.** Cover the name tag. If you cannot tell who is speaking, the line has failed.

## Same prompt, five voices

Calibration exercise, not final game dialogue. Prompt: *"A parent says the controller looks confusing."*

**Hootley**
> "She said confusing. I wrote down the word she used before that: ‘not for me.’ That’s the one I want to ask about."

**Slyvester**
> "Oh, I can fix confusing! Put a family on the poster. Grandmother, kid, dog. Nobody reads a manual when the dog is there."

**Damian**
> "Confusing usually means one button too many. I can take one out. Then I need to make sure the game still works with one hand."

**Pawson**
> "Confusing is free. Explaining it costs money. Show me the leaflet budget."

**Shelldon**
> "Mm. A controller can be simple and still look like a test. What would make her pick it up before she understands it?"

The five answers use different moves: Hootley reframes the word, Slyvester turns it into a poster, Damian turns it into a build constraint, Pawson turns it into a cost, Shelldon turns it into a question about the first moment of use. None of them should be swappable.

## Calibration tests before the rewrite

For each character, write ten throwaway lines and check:
- Would only this character say it?
- Does it contain one concrete detail?
- Does it avoid the character’s "never" list?
- Does it sound like a villager in a room, not a consultant at a podium?
- Can it be read aloud without tripping?

Only after those pass should the script in `src/core/story.ts` be rewritten.
